The invention of the printing press by Johannes Gutenberg around 1450 is often described as one of the most important developments in European history. While its significance is sometimes exaggerated, the press did transform the production and circulation of knowledge, and it played a key role in the Reformation, the growth of vernacular languages, and the Scientific Revolution.

Before the press, books were copied by hand, a slow and expensive process that limited their availability largely to monasteries, universities and the wealthy. Gutenberg's use of movable metal type, oil-based ink and an adapted screw press made it possible to produce hundreds of identical copies relatively quickly. By 1500, printing presses were operating in more than 200 European cities, and millions of books had been produced.

The most immediate consequence was a dramatic increase in the availability of texts. This had important religious effects. Martin Luther's writings in the 1520s spread rapidly across the German states through printed pamphlets, making it far harder for church authorities to suppress his ideas than it had been for earlier reformers such as Jan Hus. The press also allowed the Bible to be printed in vernacular languages, encouraging individuals to read scripture for themselves.

Printing also contributed to the standardisation of languages. Printers needed to choose particular spellings and forms to reach the widest possible market, and over time this helped fix the written forms of languages such as English, French and German. In science, the press allowed scholars to share observations, diagrams and data accurately, so that errors could be identified and knowledge could accumulate across generations.

However, historians such as Elizabeth Eisenstein, who emphasised the revolutionary nature of printing, have been criticised for underestimating continuity with the manuscript tradition. Literacy remained limited for centuries, and many early printed books were religious or classical texts rather than new ideas. The press was therefore a powerful enabling technology, but its effects depended on the social and political contexts in which it was used.
