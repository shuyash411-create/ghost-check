Policy Brief: Expanding Protected Cycling Infrastructure in Mid-Sized Cities

Summary
Cycling offers a low-cost way to reduce congestion, improve public health and cut transport emissions. However, in most mid-sized cities cycling rates remain below three percent of trips, mainly because many people feel unsafe riding alongside motor traffic. This brief recommends a phased programme of protected cycle lanes connected into a continuous network.

The problem
Surveys consistently show that the majority of residents would consider cycling for short journeys if they felt safe doing so. Painted lanes offer little physical protection and are frequently blocked by parked vehicles. As a result, cycling is dominated by a small group of confident riders, and potential users such as children, older adults and women are underrepresented.

Evidence
Cities that have built connected networks of physically separated lanes, such as Seville in Spain, have seen large increases in cycling within a few years. Seville built around 80 kilometres of segregated lanes between 2006 and 2010, and daily cycling trips rose from around 6,000 to over 70,000. Studies also indicate that protected lanes reduce injury rates for all road users, including drivers, by slowing traffic and simplifying junctions.

Options
The city could (1) continue with painted lanes, which are cheap but ineffective; (2) build isolated protected lanes on the busiest roads; or (3) build a connected core network of protected routes linking residential areas to the city centre, schools and major employers.

Recommendations
We recommend option 3. The council should identify a core network of around 30 kilometres, prioritise routes serving schools, and use temporary materials such as planters and bollards to install lanes quickly before making them permanent. Public consultation should be carried out early, and results should be monitored through automatic counters and annual surveys. Funding can be drawn from existing road maintenance budgets and national active travel grants.
