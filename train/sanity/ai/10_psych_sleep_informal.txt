Why Students Don't Get Enough Sleep (and Why It Matters)

Pretty much every university student I know is running on too little sleep. Surveys back this up: a large share of undergraduates report sleeping less than seven hours on a typical night, and many say they feel tired during the day at least three times a week. So what's going on, and does it actually matter?

Part of the problem is biology. During adolescence and early adulthood, the body's internal clock shifts later, so young adults naturally feel sleepy later at night and want to wake up later in the morning. Unfortunately, 9am lectures and part-time jobs don't care about circadian rhythms. The result is what researchers call "social jet lag," where people sleep on a different schedule on weekdays and weekends.

Then there's lifestyle. Late-night studying, social events and, of course, phones all push bedtime later. The light from screens can delay the release of melatonin, and scrolling through social media keeps the brain alert when it should be winding down. Caffeine doesn't help either, especially the energy drinks a lot of students rely on during exam season.

And yes, it matters. Sleep plays a key role in memory consolidation, which means that pulling an all-nighter before an exam can actually hurt your performance compared with getting a decent night's rest. Poor sleep is also linked to low mood, anxiety and a weaker immune system, so it's not surprising that everyone seems to get ill at the end of term.

The good news is that small changes can help. Keeping a fairly regular sleep schedule, cutting off caffeine by mid-afternoon, and putting the phone away half an hour before bed are all backed by evidence. Universities could help too, for example by avoiding very early exams and teaching students about sleep in the first week. It's not glamorous advice, but getting enough sleep might be the easiest way to improve your grades.
