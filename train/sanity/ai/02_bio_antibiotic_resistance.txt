Antibiotic resistance occurs when bacteria evolve mechanisms that allow them to survive exposure to drugs that would normally kill them or stop their growth. This is one of the most serious threats to global health today, because infections that were once easy to treat are becoming harder and sometimes impossible to cure.

There are several ways bacteria become resistant. Some bacteria produce enzymes, such as beta-lactamases, that break down the antibiotic before it can act. Others change the target site of the drug, so the antibiotic can no longer bind to it. A third mechanism involves efflux pumps, which are proteins in the cell membrane that actively pump the antibiotic out of the cell. Bacteria can also reduce the permeability of their outer membrane so that less of the drug enters in the first place.

Resistance spreads in two main ways. The first is vertical gene transfer, where a bacterium with a resistance mutation divides and passes the gene to its offspring. Because bacteria reproduce very quickly, a resistant strain can become dominant in a short time when antibiotics remove its competitors. The second is horizontal gene transfer, where bacteria share genetic material through conjugation, transformation or transduction. Plasmids carrying several resistance genes at once are especially dangerous because they can create multi-drug resistant strains.

Human behaviour plays a major role in accelerating this process. Antibiotics are often prescribed for viral infections, such as colds, where they have no effect. Patients sometimes stop taking their course early once they feel better, which may leave the most resistant bacteria alive. In agriculture, antibiotics have been widely used to promote growth in livestock, exposing large bacterial populations to low doses of the drugs.

To slow the spread of resistance, health systems are introducing antibiotic stewardship programmes that guide doctors to prescribe the right drug, at the right dose, for the right duration. Better infection control in hospitals, improved sanitation, and vaccination also reduce the number of infections that need treating. Finally, investment in new antibiotics and alternative treatments, such as bacteriophage therapy, will be necessary because the pipeline of new drugs has slowed dramatically since the 1980s.
