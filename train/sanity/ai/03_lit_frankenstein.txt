In Mary Shelley's Frankenstein, the question of who is truly monstrous is left deliberately unresolved. Although the creature commits murder, Shelley invites the reader to consider whether Victor Frankenstein's failures of responsibility are equally, if not more, monstrous. This essay argues that the novel presents monstrosity not as a physical condition but as a moral one, produced through abandonment and isolation.

From the moment of the creature's creation, Victor recoils from what he has made. He describes the "dull yellow eye" and "shrivelled complexion" with disgust, and rather than caring for the being he brought to life, he flees. This act of abandonment is central to the novel's moral structure. The creature enters the world without guidance, language or companionship, and its first experiences are of rejection. Shelley thus suggests that neglect, rather than nature, shapes the creature's later violence.

The creature's narrative in the middle of the novel reinforces this reading. While secretly observing the De Lacey family, it learns to speak and read, and it develops a capacity for empathy and moral reasoning. It is moved by the family's kindness to one another and hopes to be accepted by them. When Felix attacks it upon discovery, the creature's hope turns to despair. Its famous statement, "I was benevolent and good; misery made me a fiend," directly links its crimes to the cruelty it has suffered.

Victor, by contrast, repeatedly places his own reputation and comfort above the welfare of others. He allows Justine to be executed for William's murder rather than reveal the truth, and he destroys the female companion he had promised the creature, breaking the one agreement that might have ended the cycle of violence. His pursuit of knowledge is portrayed as selfish ambition that ignores its consequences.

Ultimately, Shelley does not excuse the creature's actions, but she makes clear that responsibility is shared. The true horror of the novel lies less in the creature's appearance than in a society, and a creator, that refuse to recognise its humanity.
